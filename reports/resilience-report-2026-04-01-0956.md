## Executive Summary

The dominant behavioral pattern on April 1 across northern border communities is one of sustained structural deficit coexisting with a broadly maintained narrative of persistence. Field reports from professionals visiting communities across the Baram, Hiram, Maaleh Yosef, Mataeh Asher, Upper Galilee, and Mevuot Hermon regions document the same core condition: residents are choosing to remain, leaders are active, and informal community activity continues — yet the physical and institutional infrastructure required to sustain this persistence is systematically insufficient. Shelter coverage gaps are documented across more than twenty communities, with quantified deficits including 55% of homes in Psuta without protection, 30% in Horfish and Ramot Naftali, and an estimated 30 households in Ramot Naftali alone lacking individual safe rooms. Elderly and special-needs residents appear in field reports as a consistently underserved population: in Granot HaGalil, Abdon, Alkosh, Beit Hillel, Dafna, and Manara, field professionals recorded that elderly residents are disconnected from welfare services, unable to reach shelters in time, or confined to isolation without social frameworks. The population-level resilience narrative is therefore structurally segmented: it is most coherent among younger, mobile, socially connected residents, and least coherent among elderly, disabled, and Arab community residents, who face both physical protection gaps and the near-total absence of formal service continuity.

A second structural pattern visible across components is the divergence between the functioning of informal systems and the failure of formal ones. WhatsApp-based coordination, volunteer networks, and community coordinators are documented as active in Abdon, Manot, Shtula, Kibbutz Snir, and several other communities — providing meaningful support for daily logistics and morale. Formal systems, however, including Home Front Command activation, welfare cell staffing, educational frameworks, and shelter infrastructure, show documented failures across the majority of visited sites. This divergence is analytically significant: informal capital is absorbing demand that formal systems cannot meet, and field reports identify volunteer burnout and role-holder fatigue as emerging risks (Shtula, Manara). The information and leadership scores (7/10 and 8/10 respectively) reflect functioning guidance and public-facing communication from national authorities, but field-level evidence reveals that this guidance does not reach its intended populations in actionable form — communities in Meiyim Baruch, Kibbutz Dan, and Goren report needing clarification of directives and public communication presence, a gap that national messaging does not fill.

## Components

**What each column means**

- **Assessment reliability** — How much we trust the **component score** (low / medium / high), based on both how strong the weighted evidence is and how broadly it appears across articles.
- **Evidence level** — A **0–100% certainty** score: how much *weighted* behavioral evidence we found for this component. **It is not “how good resilience is.”** High % means we had enough signal strength to be confident in the scoring step; the **Component score** (1–10) tells you favorable vs unfavorable.
- **Evidence base** — Number of behavioral signals tagged to this component.
- **Article coverage** — How many *distinct* articles in today’s sample contributed at least one signal, as a fraction of all articles analyzed.

**How Evidence level % is calculated (technical)** — Each signal adds `|mapping weight| × scope weight × reliability weight`. Those add up to *evidence mass*. Certainty = `1 − e^(−evidence_mass / 2)`, shown as %. It saturates toward 100% as mass grows. **Direction** (supporting vs opposing) is separate and shown under Evidence direction.

Across more than twenty communities visited by field professionals, a consistent narrative of persistence and non-evacuation is documented: residents in Goren, Netu'a, Meshgav Am, Manara, and Tzivon, among others, explicitly describe choosing to remain and frame this as an expression of community identity and coping capacity. The narrative is circulating actively through community coordinators, Home Front Command trust-building processes, and informal decompression activities. One contradictory narrative is directly evidenced in Avnei Menachem, where residents stated 'We are mentally ill people living here' alongside a discourse of staying — a coexistence of persistence and expressed distress that the dominant coping narrative does not fully accommodate.
