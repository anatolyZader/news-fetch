## Executive Summary

On March 21, behavioral evidence reflects a population operating under sustained missile threat across multiple regions, with disruptions to education, housing, and medical infrastructure documented in both the north and center of the country. Residents describe near-continuous alert cycles — averaging one every half hour in some areas — with warning times as short as zero seconds in localities such as Kibbutz Dan, producing a persistent state of tension rather than episodic fear. More than 1,000 displaced residents from Beit Shemesh have been relocated to Jerusalem hotels, a kindergarten in Rishon Lezion was struck by shrapnel, and schools in at least one municipality remain closed by mayoral decision. Interpersonal stress within shared protective spaces is visible: one resident was arrested for physically assaulting a neighbor in a shelter during an alert, a behavioral indicator of cumulative strain in confined, high-threat environments.

Two structural splits define today's picture. The first is geographic: leadership communication and protective infrastructure are more visible in central municipalities, while the eastern Galilee region — home to over 115,000 residents — operates with a single intensive care unit and recorded at least one fatality attributable to a 45-minute ambulance transfer. A community-initiated emergency medical center in Kiryat Shmona has raised over 100 million shekels but remains without formal state budget recognition, illustrating a gap between community capital mobilization and institutional integration. The second split is between life-safety compliance, which continues to function as a behavioral floor, and narrative and psychological coherence, which remain fragmented: residents are reaching shelters, but the broader meaning-making and wellbeing support infrastructure is either absent from today's signals or insufficiently documented.

*נרטיב*

**Confidence:** medium &nbsp;|&nbsp; **Signals:** 4

The dominant narrative circulating among residents on March 21 is one of unrelenting exposure rather than managed coping. A resident articulates the experience of near-zero warning time and half-hourly alerts as producing a condition of continuous tension, not a recoverable stress event — a framing that contradicts any official narrative of effective crisis management. No evidence in today's signals documents residents endorsing authority narratives as credible or reflective of their lived experience; the available accounts are uniformly oriented toward describing ongoing difficulty.
