# The Eight Components — plain language

> Plain-language summary of the Home Front Command (Pikud HaOref) community-resilience framework as implemented in `cross-cut-modules/resilience-contracts/resilienceComponents.js`. Names, Hebrew labels, and descriptions are faithful to the code; phrasing is simplified for promo and onboarding. Lineage: Fran Norris (2008).

**What "community resilience" means here:** a community's ability, during and after a crisis, to leverage its resources, adapt to a changed environment, keep functioning, and provide essential services — in order to preserve or strengthen the physical and mental health of its members.

Each component is assessed from *observable behavioral signals* — what residents and officials actually say and do — not from inferred mood.

---

## 1. Narrative · נרטיב

**The public story of the crisis, and whether it helps or hurts coping.**

A dominant narrative can strengthen or weaken a community's ability to endure. The official story has to be seen as credible and relevant, and competing narratives often coexist — sometimes complementary, sometimes in conflict (e.g. "there is a purpose to the war" vs. "victory is not worth the price").

*What we look for:* a visible, circulating story of successful coping that reflects the whole population; residents saying the authority's narrative matches their reality; or competing narratives openly undermining the shared story.

## 2. Information, Communication & Sharing · מידע, תקשורת ושיתוף

**Whether public messaging is clear, credible, accessible to everyone, and actually channels people toward life-saving behavior.**

When information is missing or not trusted, rumors and misinformation fill the vacuum. Good messaging is adapted to different sectors (language, channel, format), has feedback loops, and matches the situation people really face — actionable given real constraints (workers who can't stop, shelters that aren't accessible).

*What we look for:* residents getting the information they need and trusting it; messaging adapted to different groups; or reports of gaps, confusion, rumors, and guidance that's impractical or misses critical scenarios.

## 3. Effective Life-Saving Behavior · התנהגות אפקטיבית להצלת חיים

**Whether people actually follow protective guidelines — and have the awareness, knowledge, and trust to do so.**

Built in routine times and activated in emergencies. Depends on threat perception, clear guidelines, population knowledge and skills, formal and community enforcement, and seeing leadership as a professional authority worth complying with.

*What we look for:* residents observed or reported following Home Front Command guidance (sheltering, evacuating); demonstrated understanding of the rules; community roles promoting preparedness; or reported non-compliance.

## 4. Functional Continuity · רציפות תפקודית

**Whether essential services, workplaces, and schools keep running — and people keep their roles.**

Three forms of continuity must be preserved or restored: *functional* (roles and tasks), *identity* (sense of self and role), and *interpersonal* (relationships and bonds). The guiding principle is "help them help themselves" — maintaining continuity builds competence and reduces dependency.

*What we look for:* people managing daily life (work, commerce, social roles); schools and essential services operating; sufficient resources available; or specific disruptions (closures, evacuations, inability to work).

## 5. Community Capital & Resources · הון ומשאבי קהילה

**Whether the community mobilizes its resources — people, assets, and social networks.**

Maximizing human, physical, and social resources through coordination between community mechanisms, cross-sector cooperation, activation of anchor organizations (local authority, community bodies), and volunteer mobilization — using each partner's comparative advantage.

*What we look for:* residents volunteering or willing to; authorities and organizations activating and coordinating resources; working mechanisms to coordinate volunteers; or reported cross-sector cooperation (municipality + NGOs + businesses).

## 6. Leadership · מנהיגות

**Whether formal and informal leaders are trusted, set an example, and represent everyone.**

Includes religious figures, spiritual leaders, and community influencers. Leadership can strengthen or weaken resilience. Key attributes: public trust, personal example, the ability to channel public perceptions and behavior, and representing all segments — including marginalized groups.

*What we look for:* residents describing leadership as a source of support and security; leaders actively encouraging protective behavior; competent, professional management; or expressed distrust, criticism, or frustration.

## 7. Belonging & Solidarity · שייכות וסולידריות

**Whether people feel a shared fate and look after one another.**

"We are all in the same boat" — a collective sense of shared destiny and mutual responsibility. Includes fostering belonging, encouraging mutual aid, and providing responses to groups seen as outside the community mainstream.

*What we look for:* residents expressing solidarity or shared fate in their own words; concrete acts of mutual aid (helping neighbors, sharing resources); programs strengthening belonging; or — as a negative signal — groups being scapegoated, blamed, or excluded.

## 8. Physical & Mental Wellbeing — At-Risk Populations · דאגה לרווחה הפיזית והנפשית בדגש על אוכלוסיות סיכון

**Whether vulnerable populations are identified and given responses tailored to their needs.**

"The chain is only as strong as its weakest link." Covers mapping vulnerability and building mechanisms to identify needs and provide adapted responses — physical, emotional, and informational — for at-risk groups (first, second, and third circles of vulnerability).

*What we look for:* emotional-support services available and used by people showing anxiety or trauma; active responses for at-risk or special-needs groups (elderly, disabled, evacuees); indirectly affected people getting help; or reports of unmet wellbeing needs.

---

### Why eight, not one number

The components share a language between HQ and the field, and they keep distinct failure modes visible. A community can have strong leadership and collapsing functional continuity at the same time — a single score would hide that. (See `do-not-say.md`: never collapse the eight into a hero number.)
