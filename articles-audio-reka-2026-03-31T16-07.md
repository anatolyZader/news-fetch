- **Published:** 2026-03-31
- **Source:** reka — כאן רקע – 16:07–16:25

[Scene type: expert_interview | Speakers: poet/journalist (Alexander Elin), radio host (Alexey Demchenko)]

The guest pivots from nuclear policy to epistemology, arguing that true knowledge requires understanding, not mere information. He describes the public as receiving only 99.9% of what authorities choose to show—the 'fog of war'—while secret supercomputers process classified data to guide real decision-makers. He theorizes that political figures like Trump function as information operatives deploying calculated disinformation, while actual decisions flow from technical intelligence systems. The guest and host agree that citizens are kept as extras in a film whose plot they are never told.

Key quotes:
- "We see only what they want to show us. Nine-tenths, or perhaps 99.9 tenths, is fog of war—deliberately shown to us not as it is, because we don't need to know."
- "Real supercomputers process wind, weather, and every scenario. Those people probably know what is happening in the world and advise those making decisions."
- "Trump is not the one setting the main direction. He helps those who make real decisions by throwing the right disinformation—or maybe right information—into the space."
- "We are kept as extras, and we were not even told the name of the film, what is at the beginning or at the end. Of course we go mad, because it concerns every one of us."

## 3. reka — כאן רקע – 16:07–16:25 — scene 3: Host and guest lament information overload and citizen helplessness

- **URL:** (audio recording)
- **Published:** 2026-03-31
- **Source:** reka — כאן רקע – 16:07–16:25

[Scene type: discussion | Speakers: radio host (Alexey Demchenko), poet/journalist (Alexander Elin)]

The host cites writer Edgar Keret's observation that the flood of explosion footage and commentary creates paralysis: the more reports people hear, the less they understand. Both speakers grapple with the paradox that excessive information about threats (Middle East, nuclear risk) produces confusion rather than clarity. The host expresses a dark realization: when kept in ignorance with no answers forthcoming, citizens experience a suffocating sense of powerlessness, as though they are being dismissed like an annoying insect.

Key quotes:
- "When he watches these incredible amounts of footage, he doesn't understand where the explosions are. He listens to enormous amounts of commentary, and the more he hears them, the less he understands."
- "Perhaps it is still not good for ordinary people to know too much. But on the other hand, a very sad thought creeps in: when you are left in uncertainty, no one answers you, and you are brushed off like an annoying fly buzzing in your nose."

[Scene type: discussion | Speakers: A (male, interviewer), B (male, cultural commentator)]

A and B debate whether artists have obligations during war. A invokes the principle that art owes nothing to society in peacetime but references Artemiy Troitsky's view that wartime forces creative people to take a position. B counters that the real division is between reflexive artists who amplify suffering and those who provide psychological sedation and relief to crisis-affected populations.

- "80% of poets, musicians, songwriters—everyone who conveys a message—they all reflect. How could this happen? How did war start?"

## 6. reka — כאן רקע – 16:07–16:25 — scene 6: Artist advocates humor and relief over amplifying tragedy during crisis

- **URL:** (audio recording)
- **Published:** 2026-03-31
- **Source:** reka — כאן רקע – 16:07–16:25

[Scene type: discussion | Speakers: B (male, cultural commentator), A (male, interviewer)]

B defends the choice to provide entertainment and psychological relief during wartime, arguing that exhausted populations deserve moments of laughter and distraction between sirens and traumatic news. He advocates for shelving tragic work until after conflict ends, citing historical examples of writers who delayed publication. B positions this as ethical restraint, contrasting it with artists who immediately publish emotionally demanding work for audience attention.

Key quotes:
- "Between sirens, between terrible news, let people breathe a little, let them laugh, let them watch funny memes, funny songs."
- "It is better to write all this tragedy in a desk drawer and not put it out for public consideration until better times come."
- "Those who know they will be needed, they wait. They do not run around to editorial offices immediately."
